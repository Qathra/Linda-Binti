280		Existing response mechanisms may also require individuals to know where to seek help and how to access available services. A person experiencing immediate danger or facing a risk of GBV or FGM may not always have convenient access to information, confidential reporting channels or appropriate guidance. In addition, conventional approaches may not provide the level of privacy, accessibility and immediate digital assistance that some users may require.
281		Although digital technologies have been applied to different social and health challenges, there remained a need for a locally relevant application that could bring together prevention information, safety guidance, confidential reporting, emergency assistance and relevant support information for GBV and FGM within the Kajiado County context. Linda Binti was therefore developed to address this gap by providing an AI-powered digital application designed to complement existing services while incorporating privacy, security and accessibility considerations.
282		The problem addressed by the study was therefore the limited availability of an integrated, locally contextualised and privacy-conscious digital application that could support the prevention and response to GBV and FGM in Kajiado County. The study addressed this problem by investigating the challenges affecting reporting and response, analysing and evaluating existing digital solutions, developing the Linda Binti application and testing the developed system.

289	Heading2	1.5 Justification of the Study
290		GBV and FGM continue to require coordinated prevention and response approaches in Kenya. The national policy framework for GBV recognised the need for prevention, protection and coordinated response mechanisms (Republic of Kenya, 2014). However, individuals may still encounter practical barriers when seeking information or assistance. The development of Linda Binti was therefore justified by the need to explore how digital technology could complement existing mechanisms and make relevant information and support more accessible.
291		The study was particularly relevant to Kajiado County because FGM has historically been practised among some communities in the county and social and cultural factors remain important considerations when addressing the practice (Mwangome et al., 2021). A locally contextualised application provided an opportunity to present information and support while considering the circumstances of the intended users.
292		The application was also intended to support individuals who required discreet access to information and assistance. Features such as confidential reporting, safety guidance, emergency assistance and relevant support information were incorporated to complement existing services. The use of AI provided an interactive method of accessing selected information, while privacy and security measures were incorporated to protect sensitive user information.
293		The study was also justified from an organisational perspective. Organisations involved in GBV and FGM prevention and response require appropriate information and digital tools to support awareness, reporting and response activities. The developed application provided a structured digital approach that could complement existing mechanisms and facilitate access to relevant information.
294		Finally, the study contributed to academic and technological knowledge by demonstrating the application of mobile and AI-supported technologies to a locally relevant social problem. It provided an opportunity to examine how digital technology could be designed around accessibility, privacy, security, local context and user needs in addressing GBV and FGM.
296	Heading2	1.6 Limitation of the Study
297		The study had several limitations. First, the research was limited to Kajiado County and therefore the findings may not represent the experiences of individuals in other counties or regions of Kenya. The geographical limitation was necessary to maintain a clear focus on the social and contextual factors relevant to the study.
298		Second, the primary data collection was based on responses obtained from selected respondents through a questionnaire. The findings therefore reflected the views of the respondents who participated in the study and did not necessarily represent the views of the entire population of Kajiado County. The size and characteristics of the final sample also limited the extent to which the findings could be generalised.
299		Third, the developed Linda Binti system was a prototype and was not intended to represent a full county-wide deployment. The system depended on available device capabilities and, for some functions, network connectivity, permissions and access to relevant services. The study therefore focused on demonstrating the functionality and suitability of the developed application rather than evaluating a large-scale operational deployment.
300		Finally, the application did not replace professional counselling, healthcare, police services, legal services or other established GBV and FGM response mechanisms. Its purpose was to complement existing services by providing information, safety support, reporting and access to relevant assistance. These limitations were considered when interpreting the findings and evaluating the developed system.

987	Heading1	CHAPTER SIX: SYSTEM DEVELOPMENT
988	Heading2	6.1 Introduction
989		This chapter presents the development and implementation of the Linda Binti application based on the system design presented in Chapter Five. Linda Binti was implemented as two cooperating components: a discreet Android mobile application and a Ktor-based web service. The Android application was developed using Kotlin and Jetpack Compose and provides the main user-facing functions, including education, confidential reporting, risk assessment, Safety Compass, emergency assistance, trusted contacts, survivor support, the AI assistant and settings. The backend provides cloud accounts, synchronised reports, education content, analytics, audit services and the web-based NGO dashboard. The system was developed using an offline-first approach so that essential functions remain available without an internet connection, while selected services operate through the backend when connectivity, authentication and the required consent are available.
990	Heading2	6.2 Development Environment
991		The Linda Binti mobile application was developed using Kotlin 2.0.21 with Android Studio and the Android SDK. The project uses Android Gradle Plugin 8.13.2, minimum SDK 24 and compile and target SDK 36, with Java 11. The Android application uses Jetpack Compose and Material 3 for the user interface and Navigation Compose for navigation between application screens. Jetpack Compose supports declarative Android interface development using Kotlin (Android Developers, 2026).
992		Room version 2.6.1 was used for local SQLite storage, while AndroidX Security Crypto was used to protect sensitive preferences. Retrofit, OkHttp and Gson were used for HTTP communication and data serialisation. Coil was used where image loading was required, while Google Play Services Location and Android LocationManager were used for location-related functions.
993		The backend was developed using Kotlin on the JVM and uses Ktor 2.3.12 running on the Netty engine. The server uses SQLite JDBC 3.46.1.0 for database storage. Ktor provides facilities for routing, authentication, content negotiation and HTTP server development (Ktor, 2026). The backend also uses JWT-based authentication, bcrypt password hashing and Logback for application logging.
995		The web dashboard was implemented using HTML, CSS and JavaScript. Leaflet 1.9.4 was used for geographical visualisation, together with the Kajiado County GeoJSON file included in the project. The project also contains a Dockerfile for backend deployment and a Render configuration for a hostable web service.
996	Heading2	6.3 Mobile Application Development
997		The Android component of Linda Binti was implemented as a Kotlin application using Jetpack Compose. The application’s package is com.example.lindabinti, with version 3.0.1 and version code 46. The public launcher label is “Calculator”, while the protected application space is labelled “Linda Binti”.
998		Navigation between screens is managed through LindaBintiNavHost. The calculator is always the starting destination. Protected application routes use Android’s secure-screen functionality to prevent screenshots and recent-app previews from capturing sensitive information. The calculator route removes this protection so that it behaves like an ordinary calculator.
1000	Heading3	6.3.1 Application Launcher and Access Control
1001		The application launcher was implemented as a functioning four-function calculator. CalculatorScreen uses CalculatorEngine to perform ordinary arithmetic operations including addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, percentages, decimal input, clearing and deletion. The Android launcher label and icon identify the application as Calculator.
1002		During first-time use, the user can long-press the word “Calculator” while setup is incomplete to access the Linda Binti setup wizard. The setup process collects the user’s preferred language, display name, Access Code, SOS code, trusted contacts and required permissions.
1003		The Access Code is restricted to four to six digits, while the SOS code contains four to eight digits. The two codes must be different. Both are stored as salted hashes rather than plain text. The implementation uses PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 with 12,000 iterations through the PinHasher component. Sensitive preferences are stored using EncryptedSharedPreferences.
1005		The calculator’s equals operation functions as an access gate. If the entered digits match the SOS code, the SOS process is initiated before the protected Home screen is opened. If they match the Access Code, the Home screen is opened without sending an SOS. Any other value is processed as a normal calculator calculation. After five incorrect Access Code attempts, access is locked for 60 seconds.
1006		The Access Code and SOS code are not stored in the Room database. Device backup is disabled using allowBackup=false. The application does not implement biometric unlocking; therefore, numeric access remains the implemented authentication method for entering the protected mobile space.
1008	Heading3	6.3.2 Home and Navigation
1009		The protected Home screen was implemented through ProtectedHomeScreen. It displays the user’s saved name, connectivity information and the current SOS status where applicable. The screen provides nine main modules: Emergency Assistance, Education, Trusted Contacts, Reporting, Risk Assessment, Safety Compass, Survivor Support, AI Assistant and Settings.
1010		A Quick Hide function is available on protected screens. It returns the application to the calculator interface and terminates an active idle location session. The Home screen and navigation between local modules do not require an internet connection.
1011		The application also maintains an SOS status banner for a limited period after an emergency attempt. Where an SMS attempt fails because of missing permission, the interface provides a route to the relevant system settings. A “Send again” option can restart the SOS process.
1013	Heading3	6.3.3 Education Module
1014		The Education module was implemented in two layers: locally available educational lessons and optional online educational media.
1016		The on-device education content is managed through EducationCatalog and EducationScreen. Eight topics were implemented:
1017	ListParagraph	Understanding GBV
1018	ListParagraph	Understanding FGM
1019	ListParagraph	Warning Signs
1020	ListParagraph	Prevention
1021	ListParagraph	Immediate Safety
1022	ListParagraph	Reporting and Evidence
1023	ListParagraph	Seeking Help
1024	ListParagraph	Dignity
1025		Each topic contains learning slides and a quiz. Completion information is stored locally in the education_progress Room table. Consequently, the core lessons and quizzes remain available without internet connectivity.
1026		The application also implements optional educational media through EducationContentRepository, EducationVideoLibrary and related components. When the backend is available, the application retrieves published educational content through GET /v1/education/content. Media may be cached locally, while YouTube content is opened through external playback. URLs are checked before playback.
1027		The supported lesson languages are English, Kiswahili and Maa. A Somali option is present in the interface, but the Somali content pack is incomplete and therefore falls back to English. This distinction prevents the application from being presented as having a complete Somali translation.
1033	Heading3	6.3.4 Reporting Module
1034		The Reporting module was implemented through ReportingScreen. It allows the user to select a GBV or FGM-related category and enter a narrative describing the incident or concern. The implemented categories include physical violence, sexual violence, emotional violence, domestic violence, harassment, threats, FGM, FGM incident, FGM pressure, forced marriage and other GBV/FGM-related incidents.
1035		Reports are first stored locally in the Room database. The local report record contains information such as the category, narrative, time, area information, synchronisation status, client UUID and AI analysis information.
1036		The application can optionally determine the user’s Kajiado sub-county when location permission is granted. ReportAreaResolver maps a location fix to one of five Kajiado sub-counties: North, East, Central, West and South. Exact coordinates can remain on the device rather than being transmitted as part of the ordinary report.
1037		Pending reports are synchronised through ReportSyncManager and SyncCoordinator when a backend URL exists, the user has an authenticated USER session and report synchronisation is enabled. An idempotency key is used during submission to reduce duplicate report creation. Report narratives are not uploaded unless the user has enabled the separate report-body option, which is disabled by default.
1038		The reporting module therefore supports offline creation and later synchronisation when the required conditions are satisfied.
1046	Heading3	6.3.5 Risk Assessment and Safety Compass
1047		The Risk Assessment module was implemented through RiskAssessmentScreen and RiskScorer. It contains five yes/no safety questions. Responses are stored locally in the risk_assessments Room table together with the calculated score and risk band.
1048		The implemented risk bands are lower, elevated, high and critical. Specific responses can raise the risk level, while feeling unsafe at home results in a critical classification. The implementation explicitly describes the assessment as safety awareness rather than a clinical or legal diagnosis.
1049		The Safety Compass was implemented through AiSafetyCompassScreen, SafetyCompassBuilder and SafetyCompassAnalyzer. It combines the latest risk assessment, recent local reports and their analyses, education progress and the number of trusted contacts to produce a personal safety overview.
1050		The Safety Compass provides risk indicators, concerns, protective factors, next steps and relevant education suggestions. It can operate completely offline. Where cloud analysis is available and authorised, online results can also be incorporated. The application identifies the analysis mode as offline, online or hybrid AI. The Safety Compass does not provide medical, legal or psychological diagnosis.
1052	Heading3	6.3.6 Emergency Assistance and Trusted Contacts
1053		Trusted Contacts were implemented through TrustedContactsScreen and ContactRepository. The user can save between one and five trusted contacts, including a name, phone number and optional relationship. Phone numbers are normalised towards the Kenyan international format and are stored locally in the Room database.
1054		The SOS mechanism was implemented through SosDispatcher, SmsSender, SmsStatusReceiver and SosForegroundService. SOS can be activated by entering the SOS code through the calculator interface or by selecting the “Send again” option after a previous alert.
1055		The SOS process uses Android’s SmsManager to send an ordinary GSM SMS to trusted contacts. Internet access and the Linda Binti backend are not required for this emergency communication. SMS permission and the availability of trusted contacts are checked before sending.
1056		The SMS contains an emergency message and, where available, a location obtained at the time of the alert. The location may be represented by latitude and longitude together with a Google Maps coordinate URL. If a location fix is unavailable, the message indicates that the location is pending. A subsequent location update can be sent when an appropriate fix becomes available.
1057		The application does not continuously track the user’s location. Location listeners used for the SOS process are stopped when the emergency session ends. The SOS message does not contain the Access Code, SOS code or report narrative.
1058		The Emergency Assistance screen provides dialler links for 999, 112, 1195 and 116. These links use the phone’s dialler rather than placing the calls automatically. The application does not provide police computer-aided dispatch or automatic police calling.
1059	Heading3	6.3.7 Survivor Support
1060		The Survivor Support module was implemented using SurvivorSupportScreen and SurvivorSupportCatalog. It provides supportive messages, information, practical next steps and links to relevant assistance.
1148	Heading1	CHAPTER SEVEN: SYSTEM TESTING AND EVALUATION
1149	Heading2	7.1 Introduction
1150		This chapter presents the testing and evaluation of the Linda Binti application developed for the prevention and response to gender-based violence (GBV) and female genital mutilation (FGM) in Kajiado County. Testing was conducted to determine whether the implemented system performed according to the identified requirements and intended functionality. The testing covered the Android mobile application, backend services and application security and privacy controls. Functional testing was used to verify individual features and system behaviour, while integration testing examined communication between the mobile application and backend services. Security and privacy checks were also performed because the system handles sensitive information and provides emergency-related functionality. The testing process considered both the offline-first functions and optional online services of the application.
1152	Heading2	7.2 Testing Strategy
1153		The testing strategy was based on verifying the system progressively from individual application components to integrated system functions. The mobile application and backend were tested separately before their communication and shared functions were examined. The testing process focused on whether each implemented function produced the expected result under normal and relevant alternative conditions.
1154		Functional testing was used to verify features such as application access, navigation, education, reporting, risk assessment, trusted contacts, emergency assistance, the AI assistant and settings. Backend testing was used to verify authentication, reports, artificial intelligence services, dashboard services, education content, analytics and other API endpoints. Integration testing was used to verify functions that required communication between the Android application and the backend.
1155		Security and privacy testing focused on controls implemented to protect sensitive information. These included access-code protection, authentication, authorisation, encrypted local preferences, secure handling of sessions, data minimisation, consent controls and protection of sensitive information from inappropriate exposure. OWASP describes its Application Security Verification Standard (ASVS) as a basis for testing technical security controls in web applications and web services, making it a useful reference for security-oriented verification. (GitHub)
1156	Heading2	7.3 Testing Environment
1157		Testing was conducted using the completed Android application and its Kotlin/Ktor backend environment. The Android application was developed using Kotlin and Jetpack Compose and tested with the configured Android SDK environment. The backend was implemented using Kotlin/JVM with Ktor and Netty and used SQLite for persistent server-side storage.
1158		The mobile application used a local Room SQLite database for offline information and EncryptedSharedPreferences for sensitive preferences. The backend used a separate SQLite database for server-side accounts, reports, analytics, audit records, education content and related information. Testing therefore considered both local operation and server-supported operation.
1159		The testing environment also included the major system components used during development, including the Android application, backend API, local database, authentication services, dashboard services, education services and AI services. Where a feature depended on an external service or configuration, such as the optional OpenAI-compatible AI service or Africa’s Talking gateway, testing was limited to the implemented and configured functionality rather than claiming a live county-wide deployment.
1161	Heading2	7.4 Functional Testing
1162		Functional testing was performed to determine whether the system functions operated according to their intended behaviour. The tests covered both normal and alternative conditions, including offline operation, missing permissions, invalid access information and unavailable backend services.
1163	Heading3	7.4.1 Mobile Application Testing
1164		The mobile application was tested across its major user-facing functions. The calculator-style launcher was tested to verify that it operated as a normal calculator and provided access to the protected application using the configured access mechanisms. The setup process was tested to verify language selection, display name configuration, Access Code creation, SOS code creation, trusted-contact configuration and required permissions.
1165		The protected home screen and navigation were tested to ensure that the main modules could be opened correctly. The Education module was tested to verify access to the eight implemented topics, lesson slides, quizzes and local progress storage. The Reporting module was tested to confirm that reports could first be stored locally and that the application could maintain their synchronisation status.
1166		The Risk Assessment module was tested using its five safety questions to verify that responses were stored locally and that the appropriate risk band was generated. The Safety Compass was tested to confirm that it could use available local information, including risk assessment results, recent reports, education progress and trusted-contact information, to generate the corresponding safety summary.
1167		The Trusted Contacts and Emergency Assistance functions were also tested. Trusted contacts were tested for adding, editing and preventing inappropriate duplicate entries. The SOS process was tested to verify that the application could use the Android SMS mechanism to send emergency messages to configured trusted contacts. Location handling was tested to verify that a location was included only when an appropriate location fix was available and permitted. The system was also checked to ensure that Access Codes, SOS Codes and report narratives were not included in emergency SMS messages.
1168		The Survivor Support module was tested to verify access to support information and emergency shortcuts. The AI Assistant was tested in both offline and configured online conditions. The offline assistant was checked for continued availability, while the online mode was checked for communication with the backend when the required configuration, network connection, authentication and consent were available. The application was also checked to ensure that it could fall back to its local rules-based functionality when the online AI service was unavailable.
1169		The Settings module was tested for language selection, consent controls, account functions, permissions, optional escalation-number configuration and local-data wipe. These tests were intended to confirm that users could manage the major privacy and application settings provided by the system.
1171	Heading3	7.4.2 Backend and API Testing
1172		The backend was tested independently to verify that its API services responded correctly to valid and invalid requests. Authentication functions were tested for registration, login, device sessions, token refresh, logout and password changes. Role-based access was tested to ensure that ordinary users, NGO staff and NGO administrators received access appropriate to their assigned roles.
1173		The reporting endpoints were tested for report submission, retrieval of a user’s own reports and processing of report-related flags for authorised NGO personnel. The use of client identifiers and idempotency handling was also checked to reduce the possibility of duplicate report creation during synchronisation.
1174		The AI endpoints were tested for report analysis and assistant-chat requests. The backend was checked to ensure that an unavailable or unsuccessful AI provider did not prevent a report from being saved. The rules-based fallback was therefore tested as an alternative processing path.
1175		Education and support-service endpoints were tested to verify retrieval of available content. Dashboard endpoints were tested to verify that authorised NGO users could access aggregate information, trends, case information and intelligence functions. Administrative endpoints were tested for appropriate NGO administrator restrictions.
1176		The analytics functions were tested to verify that events could be submitted by authorised users and that summary information could be provided to authorised NGO personnel. The USSD endpoints were also tested using the implemented demonstration and gateway-ready functions. Since a live operator shortcode was not part of the delivered system, testing did not claim live public USSD deployment.
1177	Heading2	7.5 Integration Testing
1178		Integration testing was conducted to verify communication between the Android application and the backend services. The main purpose was to determine whether data could move between the two components without affecting the application’s offline-first behaviour.
1180		The reporting workflow was tested by creating a report on the mobile application and verifying that it could remain in the local database when the backend was unavailable. When the required backend configuration, authentication and consent were available, the synchronisation process was tested to verify that the report could be submitted to the backend.
1181		Authentication integration was tested by verifying that the mobile application could establish a backend session and use the returned authentication tokens when accessing protected services. Token handling was also checked during authenticated requests and refresh operations.
1182		Education content and support-service synchronisation were tested to verify that the mobile application could retrieve available server content and retain locally available information for continued use. Analytics integration was checked to ensure that permitted events could be sent to the backend without exposing unnecessary personal information.
1183		The dashboard integration was tested by verifying that information generated by backend services could be displayed through the web-based dashboard. The dashboard was also checked to ensure that it presented aggregated Kajiado sub-county information rather than individual survivor GPS locations.
1184		A key integration requirement was maintaining functionality when the network was unavailable. The tests therefore confirmed that important local functions, including access control, reporting storage, risk assessment, education content, trusted contacts and the local assistant, were not dependent on continuous backend connectivity.
1186	Heading2	7.6 Security and Privacy Testing
1187		Security and privacy testing was conducted because Linda Binti handles sensitive information relating to GBV and FGM. The testing focused on whether the implemented controls operated as intended rather than treating the application as a substitute for a formal penetration test.
1191	Heading3	7.6.1 Access Control Testing
1192		The Access Code and SOS Code mechanisms were tested separately. The system was checked to verify that the two codes could not be configured as the same value and that the Access Code protected entry to the application. Failed Access Code attempts were tested to verify the implemented temporary lockout behaviour.
1193		The SOS Code was separately tested to confirm that it could initiate the emergency process. The testing also verified that emergency functionality did not expose the Access Code or SOS Code through the SMS content.
1194	Heading3	7.6.2 Authentication and Authorisation Testing
1195		Backend authentication was tested to verify that valid users could authenticate and that protected services required appropriate authentication. Role-based authorisation was also tested to ensure that NGO staff and NGO administrators could access their designated functions while ordinary users were restricted from administrative services.
1196		The system was checked to ensure that a user’s own report information could be accessed by the appropriate user while NGO dashboard responses were minimised. The dashboard responses were designed to exclude unnecessary personal information such as phone numbers, email addresses, PINs and report bodies from NGO aggregate views.
1197	Heading3	7.6.3 Data Protection Testing
1198		Local sensitive information was tested to verify the use of the implemented protection mechanisms. Access and SOS credentials were stored as hashes rather than plain text, while sensitive preferences were protected using Android encrypted storage. Application backup was disabled, and protected screens used the Android secure-screen mechanism to reduce exposure through screenshots and similar system functions.
1199		The reporting workflow was also examined to confirm that sensitive information was not automatically uploaded without the applicable consent settings. The inclusion of report bodies in synchronisation was separately controlled. Cloud AI processing was also separated from ordinary report synchronisation and required its own consent and configured online service.
1201	Heading3	7.6.4 Emergency and Location Privacy Testing
1202		Emergency functionality was tested to verify that SOS messages were sent through the device’s GSM SMS mechanism rather than depending on the backend. The messages were checked to ensure that they did not contain the Access Code, SOS Code or report narrative.
1203		Location behaviour was tested to confirm that location information was used only when permission and an appropriate location fix were available. The system did not maintain continuous background location tracking. Where location was available during an SOS event, the location could be included in the emergency message, while the absence of a valid location resulted in an appropriate location-pending status.
1204	Heading3	7.6.5 Dashboard Privacy Testing
1205		The NGO dashboard was tested to verify that information was presented primarily as aggregate case information at the Kajiado sub-county level. The system did not display individual survivor GPS locations as map markers. Privacy thresholds were also applied to relevant aggregate information to reduce the possibility of exposing information from very small groups.
1206		The testing further examined access to dashboard functions according to user roles. Administrative functions such as staff management and audit information were restricted to the appropriate administrator role.

1237	Heading2	7.9 Limitations of Testing
1238		Although the system underwent functional, integration and security-oriented testing, several limitations remained. First, the application was developed and evaluated as a pilot-scale system rather than through a county-wide operational deployment. Therefore, the testing did not establish performance under the full volume of users that could potentially access such a service across Kajiado County.
1239		Second, the system’s SOS functionality depends on the availability of the device’s GSM service, appropriate permissions, configured trusted contacts and, where required, a location fix. Testing therefore could not guarantee successful delivery under every possible network or device condition.
1240		Third, the optional cloud AI functionality depends on backend configuration, network availability, user consent and an available AI service key. The application includes offline and rules-based fallback mechanisms, but the testing did not represent a guarantee of continuous external AI availability.
1241		Fourth, the USSD component was implemented as a gateway-ready service and demonstration pathway rather than as a live public shortcode. Consequently, the testing did not represent live operator-level USSD performance.
1242		Finally, although security controls were tested within the project scope, the system was not subjected to a full independent penetration test or formal security certification. Security testing therefore provides evidence of the implemented controls but should not be interpreted as proof that all possible vulnerabilities have been eliminated.
